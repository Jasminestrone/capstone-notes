# User Personas
**Capstone Research — Boredom, Idle Time & Phone Use**
*Based on survey responses (n=19) and qualitative interview data from NuVu students and peers, April 2026*

---

## Key Insights from the Data

**Device-free moments are rarely chosen.** The shower and bed aren't magical — they're simply the last places phones haven't colonized.

**"Mostly in control" masks habitual use.** 6 of 8 respondents who described themselves as in control also cited habit and FOMO as primary barriers.

**Boredom and creativity are understood to be linked.** Multiple respondents articulated that idle time directly correlates with clearer thinking and creative output — but struggle to protect it.

## Persona 1: The Reluctant Scroller

*Composite persona — teens & young adults who feel stuck in their phone habits*

|                        |                                          |
| ---------------------- | ---------------------------------------- |
| **Age**                | 15–19                                    |
| **School**             | NuVu / Various                           |
| **Daily Screen Time**  | 4–6+ hours                               |
| **Phone Relationship** | Uses it more than I'd like; hard to stop |

### Short Bio
This persona represents students who are keenly aware that their phone use is excessive but feel powerless to change it. Boredom is deeply uncomfortable for them; the moment there's nothing to do, the phone comes out reflexively. They've tried app limits and deletion sprints, but the cycle restarts. Many describe feeling genuinely dependent, not just habitual. Underneath the scrolling is often anxiety, FOMO, and a need for the sense of purpose or connection that the phone briefly provides.

### In Their Own Words
 "I spiral when alone with my thoughts."
 "No matter what I do I'm always scrolling something. I tried deleting apps — it didn't matter."

### Motivations
- Seeks immediate relief from boredom or discomfort
- Wants to feel connected and in-the-loop with peers
- Craves a sense of purpose when direction feels unclear
- Values quiet and real-world connection in principle, even if hard to act on

### Pain Points & Frustrations
- Habit kicks in automatically — doesn't notice time passing
- App limits and self-imposed rules haven't stuck long-term
- FOMO: fears missing messages, memes, or social moments
- Anxiety when the phone isn't within reach
- Feels trapped — knows the behavior is a problem but feels unable to stop

### Goals / Wants / Needs
- Wants to feel genuinely in control of phone use, not just theoretically
- Desires a tool or environment that makes the barrier to putting the phone down feel lower
- Wants to rediscover what idle time can feel like without it being scary
- Needs low-stakes, gradual on-ramps to phone-free moments — not cold turkey

## Persona 2: The Reflective Maker

*Composite persona — teens who actively embrace boredom as creative fuel*

|                        |                                          |
| ---------------------- | ---------------------------------------- |
| **Age**                | 14–17                                    |
| **School**             | NuVu                                     |
| **Daily Screen Time**  | Less than 2 hours                        |
| **Phone Relationship** | Uses it when needed; puts it down easily |

### Short Bio
This persona draws from respondents like Rafa, Brendan, and the 15-year-old who describes staring at walls as a legitimate pastime. These students don't experience boredom as a threat — they find it restorative and generative. They have rich analog lives: workshops, instruments, tinkering, movement. The phone is a tool, not a refuge. When idle, their minds wander productively. They get their best ideas in the shower, on walks, or while their hands are busy. They are in the minority — but their relationship with idle time points to what's possible.

### In Their Own Words
> "I love putting my phone down and letting my mind wander — it feels cathartic."

> "I find no need to 'fill' time when I'm bored. I'll often just sit and stare at an inanimate object."

### Motivations
- Genuinely enjoys the feeling of an unstructured, wandering mind
- Derives meaning and identity from hands-on, physical creative work
- Sees the phone as a distraction from richer experiences, not a substitute
- Feels most alive during flow states: tinkering, playing music, building things

### Pain Points & Frustrations
- Peers don't always share this mindset — social pressure to be on devices
- May not have language to explain why boredom feels good (and feel out of step)
- School and social environments don't always protect their phone-free space
- Limited infrastructure for the kind of making/tinkering they love

### Goals / Wants / Needs
- Wants environments that protect and normalize phone-free, open-ended time
- Seeks peers who share appreciation for making, wandering, and unplugging
- Wants tools that support making and tinkering without demanding constant attention
- Values spaces where doing nothing is treated as valid, not lazy

## Persona 3: The Aware but Adrift

*Composite persona — teens who intellectually value deep thought but drift toward the phone anyway*

| | |
|---|---|
| **Age** | 16–23 |
| **School** | NuVu / College |
| **Daily Screen Time** | 2–4 hours |
| **Phone Relationship** | Mostly in control — but habit and FOMO slip in |

### Short Bio
This is the largest and perhaps most interesting cluster — students like Munthet, Beckett, Lloyd, and the 15-year-old who wrote that incisive paragraph about friends. They describe themselves as "mostly in control" but also list FOMO and habit as key barriers. They know that deep, uninterrupted thought is valuable. Some have even articulated it beautifully: boredom correlates directly with creativity and problem-solving. Yet their device-free moments are rarely chosen — they happen in the shower, at bedtime, on a commute. There's a gap between their beliefs and their behavior, and it's closing only slowly.

### In Their Own Words
> "Enduring high-quality boredom and free thought is heavily related to my creativity and problem-solving capability — it acts like a bank of potential energy."

> "I know their habits, not their hobbies. I'm never alone with a friend — there's always a notification, always a reel."

### Motivations
- Intellectually understands the link between boredom, creativity, and mental clarity
- Wants deeper, more present friendships and real-world experiences
- Has successfully reduced phone use before and wants to do so again
- Motivated by long-term goals: better focus, clearer thinking, richer inner life

### Pain Points & Frustrations
- The phone feels easier than any alternative in the moment — low friction wins
- Social norms: friends text instead of call; group dynamics pull everyone back in
- Cognitive dissonance between self-image ("mostly in control") and actual habit
- Device-free moments happen by accident (shower, commute) — rarely by design
- Previous attempts to change (screen limits, deleted apps) didn't stick long-term

### Goals / Wants / Needs
- Wants strategies that work with social reality, not against it
- Seeks ways to make choosing boredom feel like an act of identity, not deprivation
- Needs friction-reducing design: tools that make phone-free time the easy default
- Wants peers and culture that normalize taking real breaks from devices
- Desires help building habits that outlast initial motivation
