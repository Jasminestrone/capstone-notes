# Noelle — Interview Notes
**Age:** 19
**Date:** April 2026
**Profile:** Has disabilities that make mornings unpredictable. Dinner is her only reliable screen-free moment — and only because of a family social contract. Boredom feels physically threatening; she describes it as "not being alive." Uses stimulation as a medical coping mechanism. Screen time runs 4–6 hrs/day.

---

## Section 1: Warm-Up — Daily Life & Phones

Noelle has no fixed wake-up time — her disabilities make mornings variable in a way she didn't elaborate on but flagged early. She doesn't eat breakfast. The morning is compressed into two things: get dressed, get ready. Then she's straight out the door. *"I don't really have time to do anything else."*

She didn't frame the phone as a morning ritual the way other interviewees did, but the absence of routine structure means there are open gaps where it fills in by default.

When asked what moments in her day are screen-free, her first response was: **"That's tough."** After a pause, the only example she named was **dinner** — and she described it not as a personal choice but as a family tradition rooted in mutual respect. It's more norm than rule. She couldn't identify other examples. When pushed on when she feels most digitally "checked out," dinner was again the only answer.

This is the starkest finding in the cohort. Dinner is the single daily moment Noelle reliably leaves her phone behind — and that moment exists because of an **external social contract**, not internal motivation.

---

## Section 2: Boredom — Personal Experience

Noelle says she experiences boredom frequently — *"or at least what I would describe as boredom."* Her description of it is unusually visceral.

For her, boredom is *"the state where you're not alive."* Her scheduled life — the flow of commitments, plans, and activities — functions as her IV drip. When that IV is pulled (a canceled plan, the internet going out, a task falling through), the boredom rushes in like something medical.

> *"It's almost like that's my IV, you know? While I'm connected to the flow of life, that's what's keeping me going."*

Her **reflex** to boredom is immediate and deliberate. She avoids it *"like the plague."* When boredom hits, she reaches for new stimuli — anything she can push into her head — not for pleasure, but as a placeholder until the break in routine can be repaired. She called it *"slapping a Band-Aid on the problem"* — a defensive move, not an enjoyable one.

**Frustration** arrives alongside boredom for Noelle. These two aren't separate experiences — they arrive together. Addressing boredom for her may mean addressing whatever's underneath the frustration, not just filling the idle moment.

---

## Section 3: Idle Mind — Creativity, Reflection, Purpose

When asked when her mind feels most free — when she's actually thinking her own thoughts and not reacting to someone else's — Noelle paused and then revealed something significant.

She said she uses external content deliberately to **avoid thinking about her medical situation**. *"I'm letting the world around me do the thinking for me so that I'm not thinking about the awful parts of my life."*

Her "free mind" is not free. It's occupied with keeping difficult thoughts at bay. The phone and screen use are functioning as a **cognitive suppressor**, not just a distraction. This makes her relationship with the device qualitatively different from the other interviewees. What she's avoiding isn't boredom exactly — it's the interior experience that would rush in if the stimulation stopped.

She acknowledged this is something she thinks about quite a bit, but the articulation came slowly, like she was working it out in real time.

---

## Section 4: Existing Solutions — What's Worked and What Hasn't

She tried to cut back on phone use and was explicitly **"not successful."**

Her father set up **screen time restrictions** for her when she was younger — an externally imposed solution. That's the main intervention she had experience with. The implication is that top-down controls were the tool available at the time, and they either didn't hold long-term or weren't sustainable once parental oversight ended. She didn't describe trying to rebuild that structure herself.

She had enough experience with screen time apps to have an opinion on whether restriction tools work — flagged in the interview but not fully elaborated before moving to the closing question.

> *"Boredom is the thing I avoid like the plague. The reflex is — anything new I can push into my head. It's like a Band-Aid on the wound while it's still bleeding."*

## Post-Interview Notes

**Strongest moment:** Her description of boredom as "the state where you're not alive." That's not restlessness or discomfort — it's the language of something existential. No other interviewee framed boredom as life-threatening in that way.

**Tension worth noting:** She uses stimulation to avoid thinking about her medical situation, but she also experiences boredom as a visceral threat. The phone isn't filling time — it's performing a coping function. Any intervention that removes the phone without addressing what it's covering would leave a hole that's more than just idle.

**Quote worth keeping:** *"It's almost like that's my IV, you know? While I'm connected to the flow of life, that's what's keeping me going."* — on what her schedule and stimulation do for her. Unusually precise metaphor.

**Quote worth keeping:** *"I'm letting the world around me do the thinking for me so that I'm not thinking about the awful parts of my life."* — on what her idle mind would do without input. Said quietly; came out slowly.

**Quote worth keeping:** *"Boredom is the thing I avoid like the plague."* — immediate, no hesitation.

**Contradiction:** She describes the Band-Aid reflex — reaching for stimuli as a placeholder until the break in routine is fixed — but it's not clear the break ever actually gets fixed. The Band-Aid may have become the baseline.

**Language to use:** "IV drip" as a frame for how routine and connection function for her. The medical framing is hers, not imposed. Worth holding onto if exploring the dependency angle of the project.
