# Interview Synthesis — Full Insights
*Capstone Research  ·  April 2026*

---

## Executive Summary

Five students aged 15–19 were interviewed about their relationship with their phones, their experience of boredom, and their attempts to reduce screen time. Across all five, a consistent picture emerged: boredom is actively avoided, idle mental space has nearly vanished from daily life, and every intervention tried so far — screen time apps, blockers, scheduled windows — has either failed outright or produced damaging side effects.

The problem is not straightforwardly about willpower or habit. It is about what the phone has replaced: the experience of being with oneself. For some interviewees that displacement is convenient. For others, it is covering something much deeper.

The five participants span a meaningful range. Andrew (19) has already acted — deleted short-form content and normalized phone-free train rides. Munther (17) is the most analytically articulate about the problem, yet still cannot solve it. Sam (15) is the youngest and the most embedded — she barely knows what genuine boredom feels like anymore. Noelle (19) uses stimulation as a medical coping strategy, making disengagement more fraught. Coffy (19) represents the heaviest user profile: 6+ hours daily, FOMO-driven, genuinely dependent by her own description.

## Individual Interview Syntheses

### Noelle - Age 19

|                    |                                                                                                                                                 |
| ------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Screen time**    | 4–6 hrs/day                                                                                                                                     |
| **Key traits**     | Phone separation anxiety · Boredom feels like "not being alive" · Uses stimulation as medical coping · Dinner is only reliable screen-free time |
| **Interview date** | April 2026                                                                                                                                      |

#### Morning & Daily Rhythm

Noelle has no fixed wake-up time, her disabilities make mornings unpredictable. She doesn't eat breakfast. Her morning is compressed: get dressed, get ready, straight out the door. She didn't frame the phone as a morning ritual the way other interviewees did, but the absence of structure leaves open pockets where it fills in by default.

#### Screen-Free Moments
When asked what moments in her day are screen-free, her first response was: *"That's tough."* After a pause, the only example she named was dinner, described not as a personal choice but as a family tradition rooted in mutual respect. She couldn't identify other examples. When pushed on when she feels most digitally "checked out," dinner was again the only answer.

This is the starkest finding in the cohort. Dinner is the single daily moment Noelle reliably leaves her phone behind — and that moment exists because of an external social contract, not internal motivation.

#### Boredom
Noelle says she experiences boredom frequently, but her description of it is unusually visceral. Boredom, for her, is *"the state where you're not alive."* Her scheduled life, the flow of commitments, plans, and activities, functions as her IV drip. When that IV is pulled (a canceled plan, the internet going out, a task falling through), the boredom rushes in like something medical.

*"It's almost like that's my IV, you know? While I'm connected to the flow of life, that's what's keeping me going."*

Her reflex to boredom is immediate and deliberate: *"slap a Band-Aid on it."* She reaches for new stimuli — anything she can push into her head — not for pleasure, but as a placeholder until the break in routine can be fixed. The reflex isn't described as enjoyable. It's described as defensive.

Frustration arrives alongside boredom for Noelle. These two aren't separate experiences — they arrive together, which means addressing boredom for her may require addressing the frustration underneath.

#### Idle Mind & Free Thinking

When asked when her mind feels most free, Noelle paused and then revealed something significant: she actively uses external content to avoid thinking about her medical situation. *"I'm letting the world around me do the thinking for me so that I'm not thinking about the awful parts of my life."*

Her "free mind" is not free — it is occupied with keeping difficult thoughts at bay. The phone and screen use are functioning as a cognitive suppressor, not just a distraction. This makes her relationship with the device qualitatively different from the other four interviewees.

#### Attempts to Cut Back

She tried to cut back and was explicitly *"not successful."* As a younger person, her father set up screen time restrictions — an externally imposed solution. The implication is that top-down controls were the main tool available, and they either didn't work long-term or weren't sustainable once parental oversight ended.

> *"Boredom is the thing I avoid like the plague. The reflex is — anything new I can push into my head. It's like a Band-Aid on the wound while it's still bleeding."*

### Andrew - Age 19

|                    |                                                                                                               |
| ------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Screen time**    | Reduced — deleted Reels & YouTube Shorts                                                                      |
| **Key traits**     | Self-aware · Likes boredom · "Rawdogging" the train · Strong identity · Brain "rewired" by short-form content |
| **Interview date** | April 9, 2026                                                                                                 |

#### Morning & Daily Rhythm

Ideal morning: wake up, immediately get out of bed, check the time on the phone, but only as a clock. The phone appears right away but in a limited, functional role. He's most disconnected during basketball as he physically doesnt have access to his phone and when deeply immersed in video games. 
#### Boredom

Andrew's most striking quality: **he likes boredom.** Without hesitation. For him, boredom is the absence of stimulation that creates space for something generative, he will pace around the house, thinking about projects he wants to make. 
The reflex to grab the phone is still present but he's been working against it. He described his brain as having been *"almost rewired"* for short-form content. The rewiring is the antagonist here, not boredom itself.

#### Attempts to Cut Back

He deleted Instagram Reels and YouTube Shorts instead of restricting them. The motivation wasn't grades or productivity. It was simpler: he wasn't doing the things he actually enjoyed, and the apps were making him miserable. He described short-form content as a shapeshifter:

> *"It's not even like I wasn't doing good in school. It's just making me miserable. Like, I don't even get to experience anything."*
> *"It reinvents itself. The algorithm adapts to you, so deleting one app isn't enough -- something else fills the niche."*

His approach is to full deletion over restriction, implies skepticism of partial measures. The gap between his intention and his reflex (brain still rewired, still wants to grab the phone) is the real design space.

### Munther — Age 17

|                    |                                                                                                                                       |
| ------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Screen time**    | Variable — peaks midday, right before/after sleep                                                                                     |
| **Key traits**     | ADHD · Most analytically self-aware · Boredom = "potential energy" · Most free when walking · Failed 3+ serious intervention attempts |
| **Interview date** | April 8, 2026                                                                                                                         |

#### Morning & Daily Rhythm

Ideal morning: up at 6 AM, movement, water, skincare, workout, heavy reading and study, dressed and out by 7:30. When the routine breaks, effects ripple across his entire day. His ADHD means he needs very strong structure, a lucky environment, or zero distractions, conditions that almost never arrive naturally.

#### Boredom

Munther describes boredom as *"emptiness, but it has some form."* It is his default state. Crucially, he's made peace with the duality: boredom is uncomfortable but it is also when *"there's potential energy in my brain."* The biggest flood of new ideas comes in boredom. Things recombine differently. He daydreams most strongly.
#### Idle Mind
When his body is moving, his mind becomes audible to him. *"I never really notice my own thoughts as strongly as when my body's in movement, because I get that separation."* Walking creates an internal/external split that makes his own thinking visible. Sitting plus boredom produces restlessness, not reflection. Movement is the key.

> *"Not only am I missing time to think about bigger things — I'm missing time to think about smaller things that day-to-day make my life enjoyable. I'm just missing the time to think in the first place."*

> *"Clear free thinking is one of the most important resources that we have. We don't have any other faculties to be able to deal with the world, especially how complicated it is now."*

#### Attempts to Cut Back — Three Failures

- **Screen time blockers** — worked briefly, helped build study consistency for a while, then stopped holding.
- **Isolation** — made social media extremely hard to access with many friction layers. It worked in reducing usage but disconnected him from his social circle, which burned him out harder than the phone had. Screen time went up afterward. *"The only way for me to get away from something so addicting was isolation — and that's what perpetuates the cycle for me the most."*
- **Hyper-optimized system** — built an extremely ambitious self-management framework: exact attention span windows, bandwidth calculations, full lifestyle optimization. Failed because of diminishing returns and because it left no room to simply be. *"Being able to sit and not be constantly searching for something is a skill in itself. And the phone is the opposite of that."*

#### The Social Wall

Even if you put your phone away, if everyone around you is satisfying their social, intellectual, and existential needs shallowly through their phone, you can't get what you need from people. Individual friction-based solutions fail because the social environment hasn't changed. He called this a *"spiritual and existential"* problem, not just behavioral.

#### The Tool vs. Escape Frame

> *"Is it a tool, genuinely, where you're using it to improve or automate aspects of your life that you'd rather not do? Or is it an escape - something you use to take yourself away from the things that are really important?"*

> *"An iPhone becomes an I Phone with a capital I, where your phone becomes you, and you kind of become fused with it in a way."*

### Sam — Age 15

|                    |                                                                                                                                                          |
| ------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Screen time**    | Mostly unreduced — school blocks and shower are screen-free (both involuntary)                                                                           |
| **Key traits**     | Youngest interviewee · Boredom is a knot in her stomach · Barely knows what genuine boredom feels like · "Less connected to reality" on high-screen days |
| **Interview date** | April 9, 2026                                                                                                                                            |

#### Morning & Daily Rhythm

Classic loop: alarm goes off, she picks up the phone to turn it off, gets back in bed, spends a few minutes scrolling (YouTube or group chats) before getting up. Most disconnected during school blocks and in the shower, both involuntary. There's no mention of choosing to be away from her phone.

#### Boredom

Sam's description of boredom stands out in the cohort for being **physical** rather than cognitive. Boredom for her is a *knot in her stomach*, a mild bodily discomfort, not just a mental state. Emotionally, it's restlessness. When asked the last time she was truly bored: *falling asleep.* Outside of that, she doesn't have unoccupied time.

She can articulate that boredom is "generally positive" and says she thinks of it that way, but her behavior doesn't match. She knows exactly why the shower and pre-sleep are her clearest headspaces: *"Not having ways to distract myself, really."*
#### Idle Mind

Her mind is most free when she's letting it wander — but this only happens in places where distraction isn't available. She doesn't seek these spaces out. They happen to her.

> *"I'll usually feel a little less, like, almost connected to reality when I've spent a lot of time on my phone."*

#### Attempts to Cut Back

She hasn't seriously tried to reduce use and hasn't maintained any effort when she has. She bypasses screen time blockers. Her framing of why it's hard is structural, not personal: *"the requisite addictiveness of phones and social media."* She locates the problem in the design of the thing, not in her willpower — an unusually accurate analysis for a 15-year-old.

She thinks people her age do want to use their phones less — and includes herself. The gap between wanting and doing is total.

## Section 2 — Cross-Interview Themes

### 1. Boredom has been nearly eliminated from daily life

Across all five participants, genuine unstructured idle time is either extremely rare or entirely absent. Sam can barely recall being bored. Coffy hasn't felt it in a while. Noelle experiences it only when the IV of routine is pulled — and immediately fights it off. Andrew protects boredom deliberately; he is the outlier. Munther names it as his default state but has learned to see its productive potential.

### 2. The reflex precedes the decision

Every interviewee described the same thing: when idle, the phone appears in hand before any conscious choice is made. Andrew: brain still wants to "whip out his phone" even after rewiring. Sam: distraction is automatic. Noelle: the Band-Aid reflex. Munther: first impulse is the phone, immediately. The phone has colonized the gap before intention. Any solution that relies on the user making a deliberate choice in that gap is fighting uphill.

### 3. Screen-free time is almost always involuntary

The times named as screen-free were nearly all situations where the phone physically couldn't be there: Sam in the shower, Sam during school, Andrew playing basketball, Noelle at dinner (social contract). Andrew's train ride is the only proactive exception. Most people are not choosing screen-free moments — they're stumbling into them when circumstances force it.

### 4. Existing interventions share the same failure modes

Screen time apps and blockers fail for two reasons: they're bypassable (Sam turns them off), or they create social isolation as a side effect (Munther's sophomore year). Partial friction doesn't overcome the reflex. Full deletion (Andrew's approach) is more effective but loses the legitimate utilities of the app. There is no currently available solution that reduces compulsive use without either being circumventable or costing something real.

### 5. The social environment is the hardest wall

Munther articulates this most clearly, but it surfaces across the cohort: individual change is undermined by the social context. If your social circle communicates through Instagram, not being on Instagram means not being in the conversation. The phone is not just a personal habit — it is the infrastructure of social belonging for this age group. Interventions that don't account for this will produce the isolation trap.

### 6. The best thinking happens in constraint, not choice

Sam: shower and pre-sleep (phone physically unavailable). Munther: walking (movement creates separation). Andrew: train rides (deliberate constraint). Noelle: dinner (social contract). Good thinking happens when the phone is unavailable — either structurally or by external rule. Voluntary willpower is rarely sufficient. The intervention may need to create the constraint, not rely on the user to choose it.

### 7. The phone is filling something, not just stealing time

Noelle uses it to suppress medical anxiety. Sam uses it to avoid the physical discomfort of boredom. Munther notes that the phone satisfies social, intellectual, and existential needs — shallowly, but just enough to remove the pressure to seek deeper satisfaction. Any device that simply removes the phone without providing a replacement for what it's filling will leave a hole that pulls the user back.

## Section 3 — Read Between the Lines

*These insights are not stated directly by any interviewee. They are inferences drawn from what was said, how it was said, what was avoided, and where the contradictions live.*

### The vocabulary of addiction without the identity of an addict

Every interviewee used language that would, in any other context, describe addiction: inability to stop despite negative consequences, failed attempts at control, reflexes that override intention, structural rewiring of the brain. But none identified as an addict or described the situation as one requiring serious intervention. There's a cultural gap between the severity of the behavior and how it is socially categorized. This matters for design: a device positioned as "helping with addiction" would likely be rejected. One positioned as supporting intention, or reclaiming time, would not.

### Boredom is not the problem, it's a symptom of a lost relationship with oneself

The discomfort everyone is fleeing is not really boredom. It's the experience of being alone with their own mind. Boredom is just the label they put on that experience. Sam feels it as a knot in her stomach. Noelle feels it as not being alive. Munther has intellectualized it into something he can work with, but the discomfort is still there. The actual fear is **interiority** — the experience of being a self without external input. The phone is not competing with productivity or sleep. It's competing with selfhood.

### Andrew's success is partially a privilege of identity

Andrew could "rawdog" the train and find it generative because he already knows who he is and what he wants to make. He has an inner life robust enough to sustain unstructured time without it feeling threatening. Not everyone does. Munther is brilliant and analytically gifted — and still can't solve this problem. Sam is 15 and still forming her sense of self. A design that works for Andrew may not work for people who don't yet have a stable internal world to return to. The intervention may need to help build that world, not just create space and assume it's already there.

### The hyper-optimization failure is a warning about the design itself

Munther's collapse into a perfectly optimized system that eventually failed is a direct cautionary tale for this project. Any device that asks users to track, optimize, score, or manage their attention risks becoming another thing to manage — another system that produces anxiety when it's not maintained. The best interventions in this cohort are the structurally simple ones: not bringing the phone to the basketball court, dinner with no phones as a family tradition, rawdogging the train. The design question is not how to make the smartest intervention. It's how to make the **simplest one** that people can actually sustain.

### The DoorDash insight points to an ecosystem problem

Munther's observation that delivery apps are an under-discussed part of the phone addiction loop reveals something important: the phone isn't one thing. It's an ecosystem of frictionless convenience that reshapes expectations and self-trust across every domain. When you can order food, avoid boredom, manage social relationships, and answer every question from the same device in seconds, the phone becomes the path of least resistance for everything. Reducing phone use means competing not just with Instagram but with every frictionless convenience that has quietly replaced other habits.

### The "less connected to reality" observation is the most important user need

Sam's unprompted comment — that she feels less connected to reality on high-screen days — is the most precise articulation in the entire cohort of what is actually being lost. Not time. Not sleep. Not grades. **Reality-connection.** The sense that you are living in and responding to your actual life rather than a mediated feed. If this is the thing being lost, then the design challenge is not about reducing a number. It's about restoring a quality of presence. That is a much more interesting — and more difficult — design brief.

### No one named a moment where the phone gave them something irreplaceable

Across all five interviews, no interviewee described a specific experience that was genuinely enriched by their phone use — a relationship deepened, a creative breakthrough catalyzed, a moment of beauty discovered. The phone appeared consistently as a habit, a filler, a reflex, a social obligation. This does not mean the phone has no genuine value; it means that value is invisible to the users in a reflective context. The thing the phone provides is taken for granted; the thing it costs is what's felt. This asymmetry is worth building into the design — not just reducing use, but making the cost visible when it isn't worth it.

## Section 4 — Data Visualization Ideas

### Boredom Tolerance Spectrum
**Chart type:** Horizontal positioning map (spectrum)

Place each interviewee along a single axis from "boredom as threat" to "boredom as resource." Makes the range of the cohort immediately readable and creates a natural hook for personas. Could expand to 2D with a second axis (e.g. has acted on it vs. hasn't).

**Data needed:** Sam (uncomfortable, avoids) · Noelle (boredom = not alive) · Coffy (hasn't felt it) · Munther (potential energy) · Andrew (actively likes it)

---

### Screen-Free Time Inventory
**Chart type:** Stacked bar chart per participant

Shows how little screen-free time exists in the cohort, and crucially whether that time is **voluntary or involuntary.** The voluntary/involuntary split is the most important design finding — if nearly all bars are "involuntary," that's a direct argument for structural intervention over willpower-based design.

**Data needed:** Estimated screen-free hours/day per participant, coded as voluntary vs. involuntary.

---

### Intervention Effectiveness Matrix
**Chart type:** 2×2 matrix (Effectiveness × Sustainability)

Maps every attempted intervention into four quadrants: worked and held / worked but failed / never worked / backfired. The "backfired" quadrant (Munther's isolation, hyper-optimization) is the most important for framing what NOT to do.

**Data needed:** All interventions mentioned across the cohort, rated on effectiveness (0–5) and sustainability over time (short → long-term).

---

### Where Free Thinking Happens — Context Map
**Chart type:** Icon/bubble map by location or activity

Visualizes where each interviewee said their mind feels most free. Physical locations and activities are more memorable than abstract themes — and this chart would immediately show the pattern: movement, constraint, and social/ritual contexts dominate.

**Data needed:** Andrew (pacing, train) · Munther (walking) · Sam (shower, pre-sleep) · Noelle (dinner) · Coffy (TBD). Bubble size could represent confidence of the statement.

---

### The Reflex Timeline
**Chart type:** Sequential flow diagram

Illustrates how quickly the phone enters the picture after an idle moment begins. Makes visceral what interviewees describe verbally: the phone fills the gap before intention arrives.

**Data needed:** Qualitative estimate per participant — immediate / within 1 min / several minutes / rarely — coded from interview descriptions of their reflex.

---

### Reality-Connection Score Over Time *(Cultural Probe)*
**Chart type:** Line chart per participant over 7 days

If cultural probe participants rate their "connected to reality" feeling each day alongside self-reported screen time, this chart tests Sam's observation directly. Even with 3–5 participants over a week, the pattern would be compelling for the final presentation.

**Data needed:** Daily screen time (hours) + daily self-rated reality-connection (1–10) from probe participants. Overlay both on a dual-axis chart.

---

### Age vs. Self-Awareness vs. Action Gap
**Chart type:** Scatter plot

Plots participants on two dimensions: how clearly they understand the problem vs. whether they've actually changed their behavior. The most interesting space is high self-awareness, low action — which is where Munther lives, and arguably where most of the cohort lives. Understanding is not the bottleneck.

**Data needed:** Sam (low/low) · Coffy (low/low-mid) · Noelle (mid/low) · Munther (high/low) · Andrew (high/high). X-axis: behavioral action taken. Y-axis: self-awareness about the problem.
