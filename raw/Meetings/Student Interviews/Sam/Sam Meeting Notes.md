# Sam — Interview Notes
**Age:** 15
**Date:** 2026-04-09
**Profile:** Youngest interviewee. Boredom is uncomfortable. Phone is embedded in nearly every idle moment. Hasn't seriously tried to reduce use — but is aware of the pull and has an opinion on it.

---

## Section 1: Warm-Up — Daily Life & Phones

Sam's morning is a familiar loop: alarm goes off, she picks up her phone to turn it off, gets back into bed, and spends a few minutes **scrolling**,YouTube videos or reading group chats, before eventually getting up, eating breakfast, and taking the train to school.

Most disconnected from her phone: **during school blocks** and **in the shower**. Both are involuntary — she can't bring her phone there. There's no mention of choosing to be away from it.

---

## Section 2: Boredom — Personal Experience

Boredom for Sam is physically a **knot in her stomach** — a mild discomfort. Emotionally, it's a kind of restlessness, a need to find something to do.

When asked the last time she was truly bored, she said: **falling asleep.** Outside of that — not often. She barely has unoccupied time.

Her reflex when boredom hits: **distract herself**, whether by phone, computer, music, or something physical. It's automatic.

She said she thinks of boredom as **generally positive** but **struggles to embrace it**. That's a more reflective answer than expected — she can articulate the value of it even though her behavior doesn't match.

---

## Section 3: Idle Mind — Creativity, Reflection, Purpose

She said her mind is most free **when she's bored, when she's letting it wander**. Specific places: the **shower** and **before bed** — and she named it plainly: *"Not having ways to distract myself, really."* She knows exactly why those spaces work; she just doesn't seek them out.

On missing time to think: **yes**. On missing time for bigger existential questions — where her life is going, who she is: also **yes**. Both answers were quiet and quick, without elaboration, but they landed.

She notices a real difference depending on screen time: on high-screen days, she feels **less connected to reality**.

---

## Section 4: Existing Solutions — What's Worked and What Hasn't

Sam has tried to cut back occasionally, but hasn't kept it up. She knows friends and family members who've tried — mostly using **screen time blockers** — but says those don't really work for her because she bypasses them.

She thinks people her age **do want to use their phones less** — not just herself, but people she knows.

On why it's hard to change: **"the requisite addictiveness of phones and social media."** Her framing is structural, not personal — the problem is in the design of the thing, not in her willpower.

On screen time apps: **depends on the person**, but not useful for her because she just turns them off.

## Post-Interview Notes

**Strongest moment:** Her description of boredom as a "knot in her stomach" — a physical sensation of discomfort. Most interviewees describe boredom as cognitive restlessness; Sam felt it in her body.

**Tension worth noting:** She says boredom is "generally positive" but can't seem to embrace it, and her entire daily rhythm is built around avoiding it. There's a gap between what she intellectually believes and how she actually behaves — which makes her a useful test case.

**Quote worth keeping:** *"Not having ways to distract myself, really."* — on why the shower and pre-sleep are her clearest headspaces. She named the mechanism precisely without prompting.

**Quote worth keeping:** *"I'll usually feel a little less, like, almost connected to reality when I've spent a lot of time on my phone."* — unprompted and specific.

**Contradiction:** She's never seriously tried to reduce her phone use, but says young people around her want to use their phones less. She includes herself in that group — but hasn't translated it into action.

**Language to use:** "Connected to reality" as the inverse of phone-heavy days. That framing — reality-connection as the thing being lost — is worth building on.
