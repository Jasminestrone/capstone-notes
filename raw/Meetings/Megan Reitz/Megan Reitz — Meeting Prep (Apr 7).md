## Who She Is

**Megan Reitz** is a Professor of Leadership and Dialogue at Hult International Business School and an Associate Fellow at Saïd Business School, Oxford. She's ranked in Thinkers50 — a global list of the most influential management thinkers. Her most recent book is *Speak Out, Listen Up*, and she's currently working on a new book specifically about **spaciousness and the permission to pause**.

Her core research is about **attention** — specifically, the difference between two modes:

- **Doing mode** — narrow, task-focused, controlling, efficient. You're executing, not reflecting.
- **Spacious mode** — expansive, unhurried, receptive to relationships and possibilities. You're present, not just productive.

Her argument: the challenge of modern life isn't a time problem, it's an **attention problem**. We let our attention get captured by tasks and never give ourselves permission to enter spacious mode — with real consequences: poor decisions, burnout, loss of meaning.

## What to Share With Her

### The 30-second pitch
> Modern smartphones have eliminated boredom entirely — and with it, the idle time the default mode network needs to do its job. 83% of Gen Z reports zero time "relaxing or thinking" in the past 24 hours. Existing solutions all rely on restriction and consistently fail. This project proposes the opposite: a physical "micro-gadget" that makes doing nothing feel worth doing, grounded in neuroscience.

### Survey data (21 Gen Z respondents — share the highlights)

**Spacious mode is rare and often uncomfortable:**
- Only 1 in 3 describes having nothing to do as relaxing or neutral — 62% find it uncomfortable or actively try to escape it
- 29% say their mind "rarely" or "almost never" has real space to think and wander
- 71% felt a lack of direction or purpose "fairly often" or more in the past month

**They already know when spacious mode happens — they just can't access it intentionally:**
- Nearly all respondents locate their best thinking in the shower, on walks without music, or right before sleep — unstructured, low-stimulus moments
- The problem isn't awareness. It's that nothing makes that space feel reachable or worth protecting

**The "permission to pause" problem shows up clearly:**
- When asked why they can't cut back, the top answers weren't addiction — they were *habit* and *doing it without thinking* (both at 10/21), and *nothing else feels as easy* (5/21)
- This feels less like a willpower problem and more like a design problem: there's no easy on-ramp into idle space

**Quotes worth sharing with her:**
> *"I think being on your phone gives you a sense of purpose when you can't find one."*

> *"Enduring high-quality boredom and free thought is heavily related to my creativity and problem solving... boredom = more smart and feel more open, less friction mentally."*

> *"I spiral when alone with my thoughts."*

### The core design tension you're wrestling with
A gadget that's engaging enough to pick up, but "boring" enough not to overstimulate. It can't just replace phone activities — it needs to create genuine idle space. That's a tricky design brief, and you'd love her perspective on it.

## Questions to Ask

**On the framework**
1. How does "spacious mode" differ from mindfulness or meditation? Is boredom — the uncomfortable kind — a necessary part of entering it, or can you arrive there intentionally?
2. In your research, what actually helps people *enter* spacious mode? Are there external cues, environments, or objects that work — or is it always an internal/behavioral shift?
3. Does she think a physical artifact could serve as a cue or anchor for entering spacious mode? Or is the "permission to pause" too internal for a device to really address?

**On Gen Z specifically**
4. Her work is mostly in leadership/organizational contexts. Does she think the attention crisis looks different for Gen Z, who grew up with smartphones as a baseline? Is the *permission* problem different for them — more structural, less psychological?
5. 80% of Gen Z cites FOMO and lack of motivation (not just habit) as why they can't stop. Does the spaciousness framework have anything to say about FOMO specifically?

**On the design**
6. The big unresolved question from the 3/27 feedback: *"a gadget might inherently engage you, contradicting the goal of creating boredom."* Does she have a take on this? Can an object create space, or does it always fill it?
7. Self-reflection prompts came up as a potential direction (like bullet journaling). Does she have research on whether written prompts help people access spacious mode vs. just adding another task?

**Practical / advisor angle**
8. Is there anyone in her network — researchers, practitioners — working at the intersection of behavioral science and device design who might be useful to talk to?
9. What would she want to see from this project to feel like it was grounded properly in the spaciousness literature?
