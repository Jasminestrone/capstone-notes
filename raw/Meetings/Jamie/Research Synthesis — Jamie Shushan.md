# Research Synthesis — Jamie Shushan
*April 2026 · Survey (n=22) + Interviews (n=5)*

---

## What This Is

Synthesized findings from two primary research sources: a survey of 22 respondents (ages 14–23, mostly NuVu students and peers) and semi-structured interviews with 5 participants (ages 15–19). The goal is to surface the patterns that most directly inform the design of a "micro-gadget" intended to reclaim boredom — not restrict phone use.

---

## The Core Tension

Most people in this study believe, at some level, that boredom is valuable. Several can articulate the connection between idle time and creativity with striking precision. But almost none of them can act on that belief.

The gap between knowing and doing is not about willpower. It's structural. Phones have eliminated the moments in which doing nothing used to happen by default — and every intervention tried so far has failed to create new ones.

---

## Survey Findings (n = 22)

### Who Responded

Ages ranged from 14 to 23, with most respondents between 15–19 and attending NuVu. Screen time skewed moderate-to-heavy: the majority reported 2–6 hours of daily phone use, with two respondents at 6+ hours and two at under 2 hours. First phones came between ages 6 and 14, with most getting them around 10–12.

### Phone Relationship vs. Actual Behavior

Self-reported phone relationship:

- **8 respondents:** "mostly in control"
- **7 respondents:** "more than I'd like, hard to stop"
- **2 respondents:** "puts it down easily"
- **1 respondent:** "crutch when stressed"
- **1 respondent:** "genuinely dependent"

The most revealing finding: **6 of those 8 "mostly in control" respondents also listed habit and FOMO as their primary barriers to cutting back.** They describe themselves as in control while simultaneously describing their behavior as automatic. This isn't denial — it may be that "control" feels accurate if you don't notice the time you're losing.

### What People Do When Bored

The most common first response to boredom: **open their phone** (by a wide margin). A handful said they'd put on music or a podcast, start a creative or physical activity, or let their mind wander. Very few listed phone-free responses as their *first* instinct.

### When Device-Free Moments Actually Happen

Where people report having their best ideas or most meaningful thoughts:

- **In the shower** — 14 respondents
- **Right before falling asleep** — 12 respondents
- **During repetitive tasks** (commute, dishes) — 7 respondents
- **On a walk without music** — 3 respondents

These aren't chosen moments. The shower and the bed aren't extraordinary — they're simply the last places phones haven't colonized. Device-free thinking happens when there's no other option. It almost never happens by design.

### Attempts to Cut Back

Methods tried: screen time limit apps, deleting apps, blocking YouTube Shorts/Reels, hiding phone before bed, physical lockbox. Outcomes were mixed — several worked long-term, several didn't stick. A striking pattern: the people who succeeded did so through gradual, social, or contextual change — not through an app or rule. One respondent's habit broke when scrolling simply became less interesting than observing her surroundings in 7th grade. These aren't scalable solutions. They're accidents of circumstance.

### In Their Own Words

> *"I think being on your phone gives you a sense of purpose when you can't find one."*

> *"I know their habits, not their hobbies. I'm never alone with a friend — there's always a notification, always a reel."*

> *"No matter what I do, I'm always scrolling something. I tried deleting apps — it didn't matter."*

> *"Enduring high-quality boredom is directly related to my creativity and problem-solving — it acts like a bank of potential energy."*

> *"I love putting my phone down and letting my mind wander. It feels cathartic."*

> *"I'll usually feel a little less connected to reality when I've spent a lot of time on my phone."*

## Interview Findings (n = 5)

Five participants: [[Sam Meeting Notes|Sam (15)]], [[Munther Meeting Notes|Munther (17)]], [[Andrew Meeting Notes|Andrew (19)]], [[Noelle Meeting Notes|Noelle (19)]], [[Coffy Meeting Notes|Coffy (19)]]. Together they span from the most phone-embedded to the most phone-resolved.

---

### Sam — Age 15

Sam's boredom is physical — a knot in the stomach, a mild bodily discomfort. When she's bored, she reaches for her phone automatically. The reflex precedes the decision. She can identify exactly why the shower and pre-sleep are her clearest mental spaces ("not having ways to distract myself, really") — the mechanism is visible to her — but that clarity doesn't produce behavior change.

She hasn't seriously tried to reduce phone use and doesn't believe self-imposed rules would stick. Her analysis of why is unusually accurate: she locates the problem in the design of the phone, not her willpower. She also noticed, unprompted, that high-screen days make her feel "less connected to reality."

**Design implication:** Represents the user who most needs the solution to do the work for her. Insight without agency is the defining characteristic of this profile.

---

### Munther — Age 17

Munther has a name for what boredom does — "potential energy" — and describes it as the condition under which ideas recombine, daydreaming runs deepest, and his brain approaches problems from new angles. He has tried more interventions than anyone: screen time blockers, scheduled phone windows, a full social media isolation experiment, and an elaborate self-management system built around ADHD management. Every attempt failed.

The isolation experiment was the most instructive: it worked for screen time, but disconnecting from social media disconnected him from his social circle — and the resulting burnout drove screen time *up*. He named this the **"social wall"**: individual friction-based solutions fail because the social environment hasn't changed.

Movement is his unlock. "I never really notice my own thoughts as strongly as when my body's in movement, because I get that separation." Walking gives him an internal/external split that makes his own thinking audible. Sitting plus boredom produces restlessness. Walking plus boredom produces reflection.

> *"Not only am I missing time to think about bigger things — I'm missing time to think about smaller things that day-to-day make my life enjoyable. I'm just missing the time to think in the first place."*

> *"Being able to sit and not be constantly searching for something is a skill in itself. And the phone is the opposite of that."*

**Design implication:** Illustrates the limits of individual-level interventions. He's solved the conceptual problem completely and still can't solve the behavioral one. The design needs to work *with* social reality, not against it.

---

### Andrew — Age 19

Andrew likes boredom — without hesitation, without qualification. He describes it as the space in which he thinks about things he wants to make. He has actively "rawdogged" his commute (no phone, no music, just sitting) and deleted Instagram Reels and YouTube Shorts entirely.

His motivation: not productivity or grades. Just simpler — *"It's just making me miserable. Like, I don't even get to experience anything."* The reflex to grab the phone is still there — he named it — but he's been working against it. His brain, in his words, has been "almost rewired" for short-form content.

> *"It reinvents itself. The algorithm adapts to you, so deleting one app isn't enough — something else fills the niche."*

**Design implication:** Even in the most resolved case, the underlying rewiring persists. The intervention needs to address what's been trained, not just what's installed on the phone.

---

### Noelle — Age 19

Noelle's case is qualitatively different. She actively uses external content to suppress thoughts about her medical situation — *"I'm letting the world around me do the thinking for me so that I'm not thinking about the awful parts of my life."* Her phone use isn't habit in the usual sense. It's a cognitive suppressor.

Her one reliable phone-free moment is dinner — and it exists because of a family social contract, not internal motivation. She couldn't name another. Boredom for her arrives with frustration attached (*"the state where you're not alive"*), and the reflex is defensive, not pleasurable.

She also offered an important structural observation: "A person like me who grew up with heavy reliance on virtual worlds could be equally as screwed over as someone who barely touches their phone." The relationship between phone use and mental health isn't straightforwardly dose-dependent.

**Design implication:** Any solution needs to be opt-out-able. Noelle's phone use is doing real protective work, and taking that away without addressing what's underneath would cause harm.

---

### Coffy — Age 19

Heaviest-use profile: 6+ hours daily, self-described genuine dependency, FOMO and anxiety as distinct drivers alongside habit. She can't identify the last time she was genuinely bored — boredom's generative function has been structurally eliminated from her daily life. She's thought about cutting back but never tried.

Her three drivers (FOMO, habit, anxiety) are worth treating separately. FOMO is social-fear-based — putting the phone down has a real perceived cost. Habit is automatic — the reflex predates the decision. Anxiety is physiological — the phone is relieving something, temporarily, at a cost. Any single-axis intervention addresses at most one of these.

**Design implication:** The hardest user to design for. A solution that works for Coffy has to simultaneously address social fear, automatic behavior, and anxiety relief — not just one.

## Cross-Cutting Themes

### 1. Device-free moments are never chosen — they happen to people
The spaces where people think most clearly (shower, pre-sleep, commute staring out the window) are spaces where the phone simply isn't accessible or hasn't arrived yet. Nobody said "I set aside time to let my mind wander." The cognitive value of boredom is being captured accidentally, in the margins.

### 2. "Mostly in control" is a false category
The largest self-reported group (8 respondents) described themselves as "mostly in control." 6 of those 8 also listed habit and FOMO as primary barriers. There is a consistent dissonance between self-image and behavior. The control feels real because the losses are invisible.

### 3. Restriction fails, and people know it
Every intervention — screen time apps, deletion sprints, blockers, grayscale mode — has either failed outright or produced unwanted side effects. Participants are already aware this is a pattern. The failure of restriction-based approaches is not a gap in the research; it's something the users themselves have already figured out.

### 4. The social wall
Individual solutions fail because the social environment hasn't changed. If everyone around you is still using their phone as a proxy for social connection, intellectual stimulation, and emotional regulation, you can't opt out of that system without opting out of the people. The solution has to account for social norms, not just individual behavior.

### 5. Boredom is polarized
The data shows two distinct orientations: boredom as something generative (Rafa, Brendan, Andrew, Munther) and boredom as a threat to escape (Sam, Noelle, Coffy). What's interesting is that even people in the second group can describe what boredom *could* be. They have the conceptual model. They just don't have access to the experience.

---

## What This Means for the Design

**Don't restrict — replace.** Every restriction-based approach has failed. The device needs to offer something compelling enough that people *want* to reach for it.

**Address the gap between wanting and doing.** The design has to reduce friction so much that choosing the alternative is the path of least resistance, not an act of willpower.

**Work with social reality.** Any solution that requires isolation from peers in order to function will fail.

**Don't require doing nothing — enable it.** The device's interaction model needs to be one where *not* engaging with it is as valid as engaging with it. It should make space for doing nothing, not fill that space with something else.

**Design around the edge case.** Noelle is a reminder that some people's phone use is doing protective work. The device can't be prescriptive about what "healthy" looks like.

---

## Open Questions

- Can a single device address the motivational split between the Reluctant Scroller (boredom as threat) and the Aware-but-Adrift (boredom as potential)? Or does it need to be designed for one profile first?
- What does "interaction" look like on a device whose purpose is to not demand interaction?
- Is the social wall solvable at the individual level, or does it require community-level adoption?
- How do you measure success in a pilot study when the most important outcome — quality of inner life — resists quantification?
