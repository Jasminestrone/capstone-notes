# Jamie Call — Talking Points
*April 2026 · Research update*

---

## Opening

So I've been in the thick of primary research for the past few weeks — I ran a survey with 22 people, mostly NuVu students and some peers, ages 14 to 23, and I did in-depth interviews with five of them. And I wanted to walk you through what I'm actually finding, because I think it's starting to sharpen what the design actually needs to do.

---

## The Big Picture

The through-line across everything — survey and interviews both — is this gap between knowing and doing. Almost everyone I talked to can articulate, really clearly, that boredom is valuable. That idle time is when they think best, when they get their best ideas, when they feel most like themselves. But almost none of them can protect it. And the reason isn't willpower. It's structural. Phones have basically eliminated the moments where doing nothing used to happen by default, and nothing has replaced them.

---

## What the Survey Showed

One of the most striking things from the survey was where people said they have their best ideas. The top answers were: the shower, right before falling asleep, and on a commute staring out the window. And the thing is — those aren't special places. They're just the last places where phones haven't fully taken over yet. Nobody said "I set aside time to let my mind wander." It happens to them accidentally, in the margins. Never by design.

The other thing that jumped out was this self-perception gap. I asked people how they'd describe their relationship with their phone, and the biggest category — eight people — said "mostly in control." But then six of those same eight people also listed habit and FOMO as their main barriers to cutting back. So they're describing themselves as in control and describing their behavior as automatic in the same breath. It's not that they're lying — I think it's genuinely that the losses are invisible. You don't notice the time you're losing if you're not noticing it.

And when I asked about cutting back — most people had tried something. Screen time apps, deleting apps, blockers. Almost none of it stuck. The people who did actually change their habits did it through circumstance, not tools. One person just got bored of scrolling in 7th grade and started finding her surroundings more interesting. Another set up a physical lockbox with her parents. These aren't things you can design a product around. They're accidents.

---

## What the Interviews Showed

I interviewed five people and I want to tell you about a few of them because I think they really illustrate the range.

The most interesting case for the design is probably Munther — he's 17, has ADHD, and he's the most analytically sharp person I talked to. He can describe what boredom does for him in almost neuroscientific terms — he calls it "potential energy," the state where ideas recombine differently. He's tried more interventions than anyone: blockers, scheduled phone windows, completely isolating himself from social media. That last one actually worked for screen time, but it cut him off from his friend group so badly that it burned him out and his screen time went *up* after. He called it "the social wall" — the idea that individual solutions fail because the social environment hasn't changed. If everyone around you is still using their phone as a proxy for connection, you can't opt out of that system without opting out of the people.

Then there's Sam, who's 15, and she's basically the opposite end of the spectrum. Boredom for her is a physical thing — a knot in the stomach. She reaches for her phone automatically before she's even made a decision to do it. But what's interesting is she can see the mechanism completely clearly. She knows exactly why the shower is her best thinking space ("not having ways to distract myself, really"). She just can't do anything with that knowledge. Insight without agency.

And then there's Noelle, who's 19, and her case is a good reminder that this isn't one-size-fits-all. She has some medical stuff going on, and she's actively using her phone to suppress thoughts about it — in her words, "letting the world around me do the thinking for me so I'm not thinking about the awful parts of my life." Her one phone-free moment every day is dinner, and that only exists because of a family tradition, not because she chose it. I think she's important for the design because any solution has to be an invitation, not a mandate. Some people's phone use is doing real protective work, and you can't just take that away.

---

## What This Means

So the thing I keep coming back to is that restriction doesn't work — and the users already know it. Every person I talked to who'd tried to cut back had tried some form of "make it harder to use your phone." None of it held. And the research on screen time apps backs this up — they have almost no measurable effect six weeks out.

Cal Newport has this line that's stuck with me: what you need isn't a barrier to picking up the phone, it's "a compelling reason to put it down." And I think that's exactly right. The device I'm trying to build isn't trying to block anything. It's trying to offer something worth reaching for instead.

The challenge is that the DMN — the part of the brain that handles self-reflection, creativity, life planning — it doesn't need you to do *something different*. It needs you to stop flooding it with stimulation. So the question isn't "what does the device do?" It's almost "what does it allow you to not do?" The interaction model has to be one where doing nothing with it is as valid as doing something.

---

## Questions I'm Still Working Through

I'd love your perspective on a couple of things I'm still figuring out. The first is the social wall problem — Munther's insight that individual solutions fail when the social environment hasn't changed. I don't fully know how to solve that at the device level. The second is how you even measure success. The most important outcome here is something like "quality of inner life," and that really resists quantification. I'm doing a small pilot study at the end, but I'm still working out what I'm actually measuring.
